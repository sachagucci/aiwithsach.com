Will AI Make Universities Complacent? - Nabil Beitinjaneh

A McGill educator explains how AI changes learning, which skills matter, and why job displacement and weak regulation need collective answers.

What this episode covers

Nabil Beitinjaneh, who coordinates programs at McGill's School of Continuing Studies, talks with Sach about AI and education. He sees real gains: personalized tutoring, better course preparation, and predictive analytics that flag students at risk. He also names the costs. Students can feel they understand more than they do. Personalized tools can create filter bubbles. Learning, which is often hard and uncomfortable, may get skipped. He says nobody yet has a vision for the future of education, so universities must experiment, as his own courses do by letting students use generative AI to code. The talk then turns to work. He expects AI to augment individuals but replace some jobs at the macro level, especially intermediate roles. He uses outsourcing, Uber and the Luddites to show how displacement hurts people and how hard it is to fund answers like UBI. He argues that ethics without regulation has no teeth, and that citizens rarely read the bills they are invited to comment on. He lists the risks as privacy, bias, concentration of power, opacity, and loss of human agency. For individuals he recommends AI literacy plus domain expertise. For organizations he recommends governance, clear accountability, and diverse teams.

Key takeaways

  1. AI can give you answers that create an illusion of learning, so educators need new ways to check what students actually know.
  2. Learning that is hard and sometimes painful is part of how people learn, and personalized AI tutoring can remove it.
  3. Pair AI literacy with domain expertise: without knowing a field, you do not know what to ask the AI.
  4. Ethics discussions matter but have little force without regulation, and citizens rarely use the chance to comment on draft laws.
  5. Job displacement will not be solved by one actor; it needs government, companies, schools and workers thinking together.
  6. Organizations should set clear accountability for AI errors and give people a career path, not just add the technology.
