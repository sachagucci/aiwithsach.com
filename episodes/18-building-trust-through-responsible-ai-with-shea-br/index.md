## Summary

Shea Brown, CEO of BABL AI and a former astrophysics professor, explains how independent AI audits work and why companies are asking for them. Around 2017, after Cambridge Analytica and biased facial recognition and recidivism tools, he left academia to build a firm that tests and audits AI systems with human flourishing at the center. He describes attestation-style audits, where a company documents what it has done and BABL verifies it through interviews, records, code and test results. He presents risk assessment as the starting point: who the stakeholders are, which features of the system could affect their rights, and which metrics to measure, such as bias or accuracy. He explains why bias testing cannot be cookie-cutter, how explainability helps a human catch problems like zip code acting as a proxy for race, and why systems built on large language models are harder to audit. On regulation, he says compliance usually slows innovation but is still worth it for the trust it earns. He compares the EU, US, Canada, UK, Australia and China. His advice: begin with a basic risk assessment, then align with ISO 42001 and the EU AI Act. Today, enterprise procurement is what pushes most clients to seek an audit.

## Key takeaways

1. Start with a basic risk assessment: list who the system affects, where it could cause harm, and what you will measure, then record it and act on it.
2. Bias testing needs the right metric for the context; an off-the-shelf benchmark rarely fits, so a structured risk assessment should choose the metrics.
3. Explainability helps humans spot failure modes, such as zip code acting as a proxy for race in a loan model.
4. Compliance probably slows innovation somewhat, but innovation that brings value and earns trust is the better goal than innovation alone.
5. Large enterprises increasingly require proof of responsible AI from vendors, so independent audits and certifications like ISO 42001 are becoming a cost of doing business.
6. Larger companies should align with the EU AI Act and ISO 42001; startups should begin with a lightweight risk management system.
