AI for Humanity: A Conversation with Allison Cohen

Allison Cohen of Mila explains why socially beneficial AI needs slower timelines, community-defined success metrics and wider access to AI education.

What this episode covers

Allison Cohen, senior applied AI project manager on the AI for Humanity team at Mila, explains what responsible AI looks like when you build tools for real communities. She comes from international development and global affairs, not computer science. She says that background taught her how harmful well-meant interventions can be. Her main argument is that speed works against trust. Moving fast, as Silicon Valley does, leaves little room to work with the people who will use a tool. She also argues that success metrics shape design. In a human trafficking project, usage by police would look good on paper, so the team chose community trust and uptake among survivors instead. She describes Mila's literacy programs, including ones for Indigenous students and for female-plus students, and traces the barriers marginalized groups face from primary school to hiring and promotion. Sach shares a story about AI coaching police officers to de-escalate arrests. Allison answers with concerns about emotion recognition, biased datasets and false positives. She ends with hopes for accessibility and cheaper services, and a call to stay critical of the dominant narrative and to remember the data workers whose exploitation has been ignored.

Key takeaways

  1. Slowing down is often what makes a socially beneficial AI tool work, because trust with the communities who will use it takes time to build.
  2. The metric you pick shapes the tool you build: optimizing for clicks or usage can push a project toward the wrong users.
  3. Define success with the people who use the tool; in a human trafficking project, that meant community trust rather than police adoption.
  4. Ethical risk checks need to be built into the whole AI lifecycle, with time to pause and people with ethics expertise or community representation involved.
  5. Barriers to an AI career start early, at the level of school resources and socialization, and continue through university costs, networks, hiring and promotion.
  6. Even well-meant AI for good ideas, like emotion detection for police de-escalation, need questions about data diversity, scientific validity and false positives.
