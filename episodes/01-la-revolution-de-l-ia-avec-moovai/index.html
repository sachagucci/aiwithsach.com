La Révolution de l'IA avec MoovAI

A MoovAI co-founder explains why AI projects fail on adoption rather than technology, and why the first project, governance and human contact matter.

Résumé

Sach reçoit Guillaume Petitclerc, co-fondateur de MoovAI, une firme montréalaise qui déploie des modèles d'IA existants pour des entreprises clientes. Guillaume raconte l'origine de l'entreprise en 2018 : Montréal avait une recherche de pointe, mais très peu se rendait jusqu'aux entreprises. MoovAI voulait démocratiser l'IA au Québec et au Canada. Il présente des projets pour Métro (prédiction des produits frais dans plus de 100 épiceries), Pratt & Whitney (maintenance prédictive et liste de clients à appeler) et le Collège Sainte-Anne (un outil de correction de textes qui libère du temps pour que les professeurs expliquent les fautes). Son idée centrale : les échecs en IA sont rarement technologiques. Ils viennent d'un mauvais change management, d'utilisateurs qui n'ont pas été impliqués et d'un premier projet raté, qui peut retarder l'innovation de plusieurs mois, voire de plusieurs années. Il parle aussi de gouvernance et de politiques d'utilisation de l'IA générative, d'IA responsable chez MoovAI (formation du Mila, norme ISO rédigée par son associé) et du recrutement de jeunes talents. Sur la société, il reste optimiste si la réglementation et la formation suivent, mais il s'attend à ce que les cols blancs soient touchés en premier. Selon lui, le contact humain, surtout à l'école, est irremplaçable. Son conseil final : ne faites pas ça tout seul.

Summary in English

Sach talks with Guillaume Petitclerc, co-founder of the Montréal firm MoovAI, which deploys existing AI models for business clients. Guillaume explains the 2018 origin of the company: Montréal had strong research, but little of it reached businesses. MoovAI set out to make AI accessible to Québec and Canadian companies. He describes projects for Métro (fresh-product prediction in over 100 grocery stores), Pratt & Whitney (predictive maintenance leads) and Collège Sainte-Anne (a text-correction tool that frees teachers to explain mistakes). His main argument is that AI failures are rarely technical. They come from poor change management, from users who were not involved, and from a bad first project that sets innovation back by months or years. He also covers the need for governance and usage policies for generative AI, responsible AI at MoovAI (Mila training, an ISO standard written by his partner), and hiring young talent. On society, he is optimistic if regulation and training are in place, but expects white-collar workers to be hit first. He believes human connection, especially in schools, cannot be replaced. His closing advice to companies is not to do it alone.

Key takeaways

  1. AI projects rarely fail because of the technology. They fail on adoption and change management, so involve end users from the start.
  2. The first AI project matters most: a failure gives skeptics an argument and can delay innovation for months or years.
  3. Treat AI as a tool tied to a business goal, not something to buy for its own sake.
  4. Generative AI is like shadow IT: companies need governance and usage policies before employees paste any information into any tool.
  5. Responsible AI is decided project by project, starting with whether a project should be done at all.
  6. Get help from people who have already delivered AI, internally or through a partner, because the technology moves too fast to follow alongside daily operations.
