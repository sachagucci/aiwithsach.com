The Invisible Labor Behind AI: A Content Moderator’s Fight for Justice

A former Facebook content moderator describes the hidden human labor and trauma behind AI, and her fight for fair treatment of African tech workers.

What this episode covers

Sach speaks with Kauna Malgwi, who fled the Boko Haram insurgency in northern Nigeria and later moderated Facebook content in Kenya for four years through the outsourcing firm Sama. She explains how she was recruited for what was described as a Hausa translation job. She signed a non-disclosure agreement one week into training, before the most graphic material was shown. Moderators earned less than $2 an hour, against more than $20 for similar work in the US and Europe. They had to submit a decision every 50 seconds, even on long videos of beheadings or child abuse, and were penalized for being slow or wrong. Kauna argues Meta knew the psychological cost: the work ran entirely on Facebook's own tools, and counseling was mandatory. She describes foreign workers brought in without work permits and contracts moving from Kenya to Ghana and Nigeria to avoid accountability. She also covers her testimony at the European Parliament on the Platform Work Directive. A psychiatric evaluation of about 140 petitioners found PTSD in nearly all of them. Two years after the layoffs, the court case against Meta is still stuck on jurisdiction. Her core message: AI did not build itself, and the people who trained it need care and recognition.

Key takeaways

  1. AI and social media safety depend on human moderators, often in the Global South, who are rarely acknowledged as part of how AI is built.
  2. Deceptive recruitment and early NDAs removed moderators' ability to make an informed choice about traumatic work; transparency is the first fix Kauna asks for.
  3. Productivity quotas (one decision every 50 seconds) and pay under $2/hour turn moderation into what Kauna calls forced labor.
  4. Outsourcing lets platforms deny responsibility, but the work runs on their own systems, and mandatory counseling shows they know the harm.
  5. When legal pressure rises, contracts move to countries with weaker labor laws (Kenya to Ghana to Nigeria), which is why Kauna calls on the African Union to act.
  6. Moderator trauma is ongoing, not a single event; care models must address repeated exposure, stigma at home and blacklisting after the job.
