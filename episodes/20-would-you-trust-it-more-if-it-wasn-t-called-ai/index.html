Would you trust it more if it wasn't called AI?

A former PayPal innovation lead explains why the name 'AI' makes people wary, and why ordinary people and small businesses should just start using it.

What this episode covers

Mike Todasco, who ran the innovation group at PayPal, talks with Sach about a survey he ran on how the label 'AI' changes what people think of a tool. He asked 200 people about driving directions, calling it 'AI' for one half and 'Google Maps' for the other. The AI wording scored 0.5 to 2 points worse on a seven-point scale. Mike traces the name back to the 1950s Dartmouth workshop, where 'artificial intelligence' won because it sounded futuristic and helped with funding. He prefers 'augmented intelligence' and describes today's models as very good autocomplete that works as a tool, not a separate being. He explains why attitudes differ between Japan and the United States, pointing to social media as a source of disappointment in the US. He also names risks: models built to please us, and people getting attached to systems that sound human. His advice is simple. Use the tools, be open about how you use them, and publish a policy on what you will and will not do with AI. He is most excited about small businesses and individuals who can now build software for themselves.

Key takeaways

  1. The word 'AI' alone can lower how favourably people rate a tool: in Mike's survey, the same driving-directions product scored 0.5 to 2 points worse on a seven-point scale when called AI.
  2. Treating AI as augmented intelligence, a tool in your workflow, is more accurate and less frightening than treating it as a separate being.
  3. Be transparent: label AI-generated images with the model, date and prompt, and publish an internal and external policy on what you will and won't use AI for.
  4. Small businesses and startups gain the most: for about $20 a month they get capabilities that once required hiring specialists.
  5. Start by using the tools daily, make one the default in your browser and phone, and ask the AI itself how to begin.
  6. Keep the downsides in view: models tend to agree with you, and people may bond with systems that sound human.
