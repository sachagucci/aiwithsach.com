## Summary

Sach talks with Alain Filotto, a retired RCMP officer with 29 years in policing and now a Digital Evidence Specialist at Alphafox Forensics. Alain starts with his career, from pagers and BlackBerrys to child exploitation and national security investigations. His main point is that most cybercrime is old-fashioned fraud that is now faster and reaches more people. AI makes it harder to spot because the broken grammar that used to give scams away is gone. They discuss who is responsible when an employee falls for a phishing email, and why Alain thinks Google should not take ad money from scammers. They cover how police use AI, from identifying victims to analysing cameras, and the ethical limits of surveillance, using the Paris Olympics as an example. Alain argues that private forensic firms will take on more work, and that international cooperation remains slow. He warns that using AI to detect fakes also teaches it to make better ones. On open source versus closed AI, he thinks you cannot put the genie back in the bottle. His advice is practical: do not click links you did not expect, and verify everything before you react.

## Key takeaways

1. Most cybercrime is traditional fraud made faster and cheaper, so the basic rules still apply: if you did not expect a payment request or a link, do not act on it.
2. AI removes the old tells of scams, like bad grammar, so people need verification habits instead of relying on spotting mistakes.
3. Companies share responsibility for fraud: they should filter threats, train staff, and test them with fake phishing emails.
4. Using AI to detect fakes also feeds the models that make fakes, so detection tools are never a final answer.
5. Policing is a cat-and-mouse game, and slow cross-border cooperation and lab backlogs push work toward the private sector.
6. The most useful skill against AI-enabled fraud is the habit of questioning and double-checking before reacting.
