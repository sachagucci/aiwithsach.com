The Real Difference Between Data, ML, and AI Engineers

Og Ramos explains what data, ML and AI engineers actually do, why roles are blurring, and why curiosity and fundamentals matter more than any tool.

What this episode covers

Sach talks with Og Ramos, an engineer with 25 years in tech and about 10 in data, now a consultant. Og lays out how data engineers, software engineers, data scientists, ML engineers and AI engineers differ. Software engineers sit on the transactional side. Data engineers move and shape the data. Data scientists work on the semantic layer. ML engineers train models. AI engineers build end-to-end solutions with prompts, RAG and guardrails. He stresses that these definitions depend on the company and its maturity. They discuss what a good team looks like and why Og would add a data ops role. Og argues that everybody will need to be a prompt engineer, that generalists with some depth do better, and that people should learn how code actually works rather than the syntax. For a company starting out with AI, he says to hire a data engineer first and build the foundation, since a dashboard on bad data is still bad data. They also cover lower barriers for entrepreneurs, trust and regulation, personal brand, and the idea of a personal LLM acting as an apprentice. Og's advice for companies is to feed internal knowledge into an LLM. His closing advice for listeners is to stay curious and follow what they love.

Key takeaways

  1. Role definitions for data, ML and AI engineers vary by company and maturity, so ask what the job actually involves rather than trusting the title.
  2. If a company has messy data and wants to start with AI, the first hire is a data engineer who builds the foundation, not a data scientist.
  3. Everyone will need basic prompt skills: give the model a role, context, the task and the best practices you want applied.
  4. Learn why code works (execution, architecture, patterns) rather than only a language, because tools and languages change.
  5. Being a generalist with some depth and the curiosity to ask good questions is what lets people and teams adapt.
  6. Companies can get a lot from centralizing internal and tribal knowledge into an LLM they control.
