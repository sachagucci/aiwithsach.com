## Summary

Sach talks with Bishop Thomas Dowd, who grew up in Montréal, wrote his first program at 12, worked at Ericsson, became webmaster for the Canadian bishops in 1995 and was named a bishop at 40. He now leads a diocese in Northern Ontario. Dowd treats large language models as tools. He has caught ChatGPT filling in blanks, and he describes how an AI trained on Catholic theology gave a parishioner a wrong answer about what the diocese could do. He argues that a machine cannot pray for you. On consciousness, he sets out the Catholic view of the soul, the role of the body in knowing, and free will. He says AI will force us to ask what a soul is. He reads AI boyfriends and girlfriends as a symptom of loneliness that urbanization and social media already worsened, and he warns of a society split by emotional capacity. He wants profit-driven companies regulated, as airplanes and hot dogs are. He ends on honesty and altruism as the virtues we need, and on what a church can offer that cannot be virtualized.

## Key takeaways

1. Treat chatbots as a starting point for research, not a trusted source: check the answer against something you know or a person who knows.
2. An AI is only as good as its training inputs, and copyright limits can keep it stuck on older, worse material.
3. The questions about AI consciousness force a prior question: what do we mean by consciousness, free will or a soul?
4. AI companions meet real loneliness, and companies may profit from it; the risk is people avoiding the hard, risky work of human relationships.
5. Regulation of profit-driven AI makes sense for the same reasons we regulate airplanes, vaccines and meat, and the real danger is people using AI without empathy.
6. Honesty and altruism matter more as technology magnifies their presence or absence.
