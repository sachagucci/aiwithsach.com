## Summary

Sach talks with Avinash and Ajith, co-founders of Cactus, an AI business assistant for solopreneurs such as private chefs, personal trainers and mechanics. The idea started with their own lives: both became fathers and hired private chefs. They started a marketplace called Craved. Then they found that chefs had no trouble finding clients. The trouble was admin work, and some chefs had no idea what they really earned. They pivoted to a tool that builds a branded website, qualifies inquiries by voice and handles bookings. Avinash explains his choices: a Ruby on Rails monolith, building slowly, and serving needs rather than wants. Ajith describes what Y Combinator gave them, speed, focus and belief, and why a co-founder relationship rests on trust. They say the hardest part is deciding what to build, since building is now easy. They also keep 10% of the work unautomated, the part that carries the owner's personality. Ajith says what worries him is AI replacing human relationships. Avinash is excited that solo founders can now ship real software.

## Key takeaways

1. Watch what customers need, not only what they ask for. Group repeated requests into patterns and solve ten problems well instead of a hundred poorly.
2. Talk to at least three users every day. Cactus started by looking at a chef's real transactions, which showed she earned half of what she thought.
3. With AI making code cheap, the hard part is deciding what to build, not building it.
4. Choose boring, productive tools such as a Rails monolith, and use AI only where it delivers value, with controls left to the customer.
5. Decide in advance what not to automate. Cactus keeps the owner's personality and relationships as the 10% it leaves alone.
6. A co-founder relationship depends on trust and shared direction first. Division of work comes second, with clear accountability.
